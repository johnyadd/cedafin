Ghanaians abroad sent home about **US$7.8 billion in 2025** — roughly 6% of GDP,
and more than the country received in foreign direct investment. Almost all of
it was spent.

Bank of Ghana would like some of it invested instead. Its Remit2Invest
initiative, launched in April 2026, treats the diaspora as domestic investors
abroad, and the Securities and Exchange Commission has been pressing market
operators to build products for them. The SEC's Deputy Director General for
Finance has put the potential at around US$4 billion.

The intent is there. What is missing is the information anyone would need to
act on it.

## The problem that only applies to you

A Ghanaian in Accra investing cedis in a cedi fund has one question: what does
it return?

You have two. What does it return, and what does the cedi do against the pound
or the dollar while your money is in it? The second usually matters more.

Here is what that looks like with real figures. Between 1 June and 28 August
2026:

| | Move |
|---|---|
| Gold, in dollars | +0.51% |
| The cedi, against the dollar | strengthened 11.735 to 11.215 |
| **The Ghana Gold Coin, in cedis** | **−3.93%** |

Gold rose. The coin fell. Nothing happened to the metal — the currency moved,
and a strengthening cedi works against anyone holding a dollar-priced asset.

**Now reverse it.** A saver in London holding a cedi money market fund over the
same period would have earned the fund's return *and* gained from the cedi
strengthening, because their cedis converted back into more pounds.

The currency giveth and taketh, and which it does depends entirely on when you
send and when you take it out. No fund factsheet mentions this, because for a
domestic investor it does not arise.

## What that means in practice

**A cedi return is not your return.** A fund reporting 33% in cedis delivered
something different to you, depending on where the exchange rate went. Over a
year when the cedi weakens 20%, a 33% cedi return is roughly 6% in pounds.
Over a year when it strengthens, the same return is worth considerably more.

**Timing the transfer matters as much as picking the fund.** Nobody selling you
a fund will say this, because it is not their business — but the exchange rate
on the day you send often moves your outcome more than the difference between
the cheapest fund and the dearest.

**And foreign currency options exist, unpublished.** Several Ghanaian banks
offer dollar and pound deposit accounts. Not one publishes comparable rates for
them. We have asked GCB directly, because their own deposit table does not even
state which currency it refers to.

## What you can actually reach from abroad

This is where honesty gets uncomfortable, because much of it is unknown.

**Funds.** Most Ghanaian fund managers accept applications from abroad in
principle. What they need from a non-resident — which documents, whether a
Ghanaian bank account is required, how long it takes — is not published by any
of the managers we track. You will have to ask each one.

**Treasury bills.** Through a Ghanaian bank or broker. Same question, same lack
of an answer.

**Listed shares.** Through one of twenty-four licensed dealing members. **We
checked every one of their websites in August 2026 and not one publishes a
commission rate.** One publishes a minimum to open an account. Six have no
working website at all. Whether any of them will open an account for someone
resident abroad is not stated anywhere, by anyone.

**Gold.** The Ghana Gold Coin is bought through a commercial bank, and Bank of
Ghana states that purchases must be in cedis — foreign currency payment is not
permitted for residents. Whether non-residents are treated differently is not
addressed. The NewGold ETF trades on the exchange at around GH₵462 a unit and
needs a broker, which returns you to the paragraph above.

## The question nobody publishes an answer to

**Can I open an account without coming to Ghana?**

For someone in London, Toronto or New York, that is the first question and the
one that decides everything. Not the charge, not the return — whether the thing
is reachable at all.

We have asked every broker and every fund manager whose contact details we
hold. Not one currently publishes the answer. When any of them replies, it goes
on their page, cited and dated.

Until then the honest advice is unglamorous: contact them directly, ask what a
non-resident needs, and ask before you send money rather than after.

## Three things worth doing regardless

**Ask for the figure in your currency, or work it out.** A cedi return means
nothing to you until you convert it at both ends. Any manager quoting a return
should be able to tell you the period it covers, and you can check what the
exchange rate did over the same window.

**Ask what happens when you want it back.** Getting money into Ghana is easy;
getting it out is where the questions arise. Ask about repatriation before you
commit, not after.

**And compare before you send.** The cheapest Ghanaian fund we can verify
charges 1.75% a year and the dearest 2.25%. That gap is small beside the
currency movement — which is precisely why it is worth not also paying more
than you need to.
