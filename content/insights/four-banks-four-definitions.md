Somebody in London who wants a bank account in Ghana has a short list of
questions. Will they take me? Do I have to fly there? What do I need to send?

Four Ghanaian banks answer all three in public, and they give four different
answers. What one will accept by email, another wants certified by a lawyer.

## Who counts as diaspora

Start with the question that decides whether the rest matters.

**Zenith** are the narrowest and the clearest. The applicant must be a
Ghanaian resident abroad, and 18 or over. That is the whole of it.

**GCB** are the widest by a long way. Their Link2Home account is open to
Ghanaians resident abroad; to Ghanaians by birth who have since acquired
another country's citizenship; to persons born to at least one Ghanaian
parent; and to a foreigner married to a Ghanaian, on a joint account.

That third category is the one to notice. A woman born in Croydon to a
Ghanaian mother, who has never held a Ghanaian passport and may never have
been to Ghana, qualifies at GCB. She does not, on the published terms, qualify
at Zenith.

**Absa** and **Fidelity** both say "Ghanaians living abroad" and leave it
there. Which is not a complaint — most applicants know whether that describes
them — but it does mean the second-generation question has no published
answer at either.

## Where you have to be standing

Here the ordering reverses.

**Zenith** state that the account may be opened and operated from anywhere in
the world. **Fidelity** describe theirs as a way to bank securely from
anywhere in the world.

**GCB**, the bank with the most generous view of who counts, publish three
conditions that point the other way. The account is opened with an initial
deposit — and the page adds, in brackets, "(when in Ghana)". Savings account
holders must be present at the branch to make over-the-counter withdrawals.
And a newly opened diasporan account must be funded within 14 days.

**Absa** do not address it. They have an online application, a "resume an
application" button, and a team of dedicated diaspora consultants. None of
that is a statement that the process can be completed without travelling, and
we do not read it as one.

So the bank that will take your mother's nationality appears to want you in a
branch, and the bank that wants your own passport will deal with you by email.

## What paper you need

Three of the four require a document that has to be obtained where the
applicant lives, at the applicant's expense. None of them says what it costs,
which is fair enough — it is not their charge.

**Absa** require certification of documents by a lawyer, a notary public, or a
court of competent jurisdiction. It is mandatory for Ghanaians living abroad,
on every account type, and the person certifying must be known and capable of
being contacted.

**Fidelity** require a Power of Attorney where a mortgage applicant is
non-resident. Their account opening form also specifies identification as
"ONLY Valid National I.D", which is narrower than the passport-or-card the
others accept.

**EDC Stockbrokers**, who are not a bank but sit on the same page of this
site, require copies notarised by a foreign authority.

**Zenith** require a downloaded form, one passport photograph, and a copy of a
Ghana Card or valid international Ghanaian passport — emailed to their
diaspora customer service address. No notary, no certification, no lawyer.

That last one is what makes the others a finding rather than a complaint. The
paperwork is not a regulatory floor that every bank must meet. One of them
does without it.

## What it costs to open

**Absa** require no minimum balance on the current account, and charge GHS
21.50 a month.

**Fidelity** require 100 — in cedis, dollars, pounds or euros, with no
requirement to open a cedi account first — charge no monthly maintenance fee
on any currency, and pay 2% a year on the cedi balance.

**Zenith** require GHS 500 to open and GHS 200 maintained. Their foreign
currency accounts sit behind a cedi account, which must be opened first.

**GCB** publish that the account is operated with a minimum balance, without
saying what it is.

On the published terms Fidelity is the cheapest way in and the only one paying
anything. But a notary in the United Kingdom charges more per document than
Zenith's entire opening balance — so for somebody who would have to pay for
certification, Absa's account with no minimum may cost the most to open.

## The thing none of them can tell you

Every one of these terms is published. None of the four banks is hiding
anything, and none of them is wrong.

But a bank can only tell you its own terms. It cannot tell you that the
competitor down the road will take your documents by email while it wants them
certified by a lawyer, or that the one with no minimum balance may cost more
to open than the one asking for GHS 500.

That comparison has to be made by somebody outside all four, and until it is,
a Ghanaian abroad chooses on whichever site they happened to land on.

## One more thing, which may matter more than all of it

Ghana's National Identification Authority states that the Ghana Card is
mandatory for transactions requiring an applicant to establish their identity,
and names banking among the services it covers.

All four banks currently accept a passport instead. Ghanaians abroad can now
register for the card at Ghana Missions. Whether the passport remains
acceptable indefinitely is a question worth putting to whichever bank you
approach, rather than discovering the answer later.

---

**One thing this piece does not cover.** First National Bank publish four
home loan products, each available to resident and non-resident Ghanaians in
cedis, dollars or pounds, over up to twenty years. That is lending rather than
an account to hold money in, so it sits outside the comparison above — but for
somebody abroad buying property in Ghana it may matter more than any of it.
