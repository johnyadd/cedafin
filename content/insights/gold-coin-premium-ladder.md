Bank of Ghana publishes a price for the Ghana Gold Coin every working day by
9am. Three denominations, one figure each, derived from the previous day's LBMA
gold price and the Bloomberg USD/GHS rate. On 28 August 2026 they read:

| Denomination | Price |
|---|---|
| 1.00 oz | GH₵53,013.65 |
| 0.50 oz | GH₵26,857.84 |
| **0.25 oz** | GH₵13,802.71 |

Nothing there looks unusual. Half an ounce costs about half; a quarter costs
about a quarter. The circular prints no commentary and no comparison, and there
is no reason a reader would do arithmetic on it.

But the same circular carries the two figures the price was built from: the
LBMA gold price of $4,568.95 an ounce, and an exchange rate of 11.2150. Multiply
them and an ounce of gold was worth about **GH₵51,241** that day.

## What each coin costs above the metal in it

| Denomination | Price | Metal worth | Above the metal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 oz | GH₵53,013.65 | GH₵51,240.77 | **3.46%** |
| 0.50 oz | GH₵26,857.84 | GH₵25,620.38 | **4.83%** |
| **0.25 oz** | GH₵13,802.71 | GH₵12,810.19 | **7.75%** |

The premium is what pays for minting, the central bank's guarantee and
distribution. It is a real and reasonable cost. What is less obvious is that it
more than doubles as the coin gets smaller.

Buy an ounce of gold in four quarter-ounce pieces and it costs **GH₵55,211**
against **GH₵53,014** for a single coin — about **GH₵2,197 more** for the same
quantity of the same metal.

## Who pays it

The person who cannot afford a full ounce.

A saver with GH₵55,000 can buy the one-ounce coin and pay 3.46%. A saver with
GH₵14,000 has one option, and it costs 7.75%. The pricing is not aimed at
anyone — it presumably reflects that minting four small coins costs more than
minting one large one — but the effect is that the cost of owning gold in Ghana
rises as the buyer's means fall.

That pattern is not confined to gold. It is the same shape as fund minimums,
where the cheapest charges sit behind the highest entry thresholds, and as SME
lending, where the smallest borrowers pay the widest spreads.

## And the entry price is the larger barrier

The cheapest way into the Ghana Gold Coin is GH₵13,803.

Stanbic Cash Trust accepts GH₵20. Gold is recommended to ordinary savers
everywhere as protection against inflation and currency weakness, and in Ghana
the cheapest coin costs **690 times** what the cheapest money market fund asks
for.

There is one alternative. The NewGold ETF trades on the Ghana Stock Exchange at
about **GH₵462 a unit** — roughly one hundredth of an ounce, backed by physical
bullion. Thirty times cheaper to start than the smallest coin.

It is charged differently, though, and that matters more than it first appears.

## A premium paid once, against a fee paid every year

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| Ghana Gold Coin, 1 oz | 3.58% once, on purchase |
| **Ghana Gold Coin, ¼ oz** | 7.75% once, on purchase |
| NewGold ETF | 0.30% every year |

These cannot be compared without a holding period. Divide one by the other and
you get the year at which they cross:

- Against the **one-ounce coin**, the ETF is cheaper for **12 years**
- Against the **half-ounce**, **16 years**
- Against the **quarter-ounce**, **26 years**

Hold gold longer than that and the coin wins. Hold it for less — which is most
people, most of the time — and the ETF is cheaper, dramatically so for anyone
who would otherwise buy the small coin.

Neither figure includes what a Ghanaian stockbroker charges to buy the ETF.
[Not one of the twenty-four licensed dealing members publishes a
rate](/brokers).

## What gold actually did

There is one more thing the daily circulars show, and it is the least
comfortable.

Between 1 June and 28 August 2026, gold rose **0.51%** in dollars. The Ghana
Gold Coin fell **3.93%** in cedis.

The difference is the currency. The cedi strengthened from 11.735 to 11.215
against the dollar over those months, and a strengthening currency works
against anyone holding a dollar-priced asset. Add the premium paid on purchase
and a buyer who bought a one-ounce coin on 1 June was down about **7.5%** by
the end of August.

Gold is bought as protection against a falling currency. Over that period the
cedi rose, so it guarded against something that did not happen — and the
premium was paid either way.

That is not an argument against gold. It is an argument for knowing what you
are buying, which requires figures nobody had put side by side until now.
